# The published-guidance basis for our content criteria

**Purpose.** The evidence behind `content-screen`'s criteria, in Google's own published wording — so a
content decision can be settled by checking a source rather than by whoever argues hardest. Screening
rules are `content-screen`; the standing principle both docs serve is `ia` §"Guiding principles".
**Doc-key: `content-vocab` · Species: living reference** — the Google citations are current as of
2026-08-05 and need a recency check before being relied on later.

**What this is for.** "This feels cheap and generic" is often a correct reaction, but it's a judgment,
and judgments don't settle disagreements between people who both know their field. What follows is
Google's own documentation instead. Two consequences, and the second matters as much as the first:

1. A criterion in `content-screen` that can be traced to a line here is a shared standard, not a
   preference — design isn't overruling SEO or vice versa, both are reading the same source.
2. **A criterion that can't be traced here should be changed.** If someone brings a dated Google source
   or Search Console data that contradicts something below, the doc is wrong and gets rewritten. That
   cuts against design taste as readily as against old SEO habit.

**Useful way to open a specific disagreement:** *"Let's check this against the spam policy and the
helpful-content page rather than arguing style — which line are we each reading?"*

**"Where it applies" notes below** cite the SEO team's 2026-08-05 deliverables — the sub-service
description doc (36 templated blurbs) and the AI Transformation page draft. Later deliverables need
their own read; don't recycle the specifics.

## 0. Lead with this one — it settles the argument before it starts

Everything below is useful for the execution-level fight. But there's a stronger argument sitting
above all of it, and it doesn't require winning an SEO technical debate at all: **it's a
brand-coherence problem, not a tactics problem.**

VECTO's positioning bet is specifically that it knows how to use AI without the output degrading
into [AI slop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_slop) — content that's fluent-sounding but empty.
The actual definition of AI slop, direct from the sources: *"superficial competence… belied by a
lack of underlying substance, craft, or communicative intent"*; *"generic, repetitive… little
unique insight or value"*; produced at volume with the goal of "attention and reach," not genuine
communication. (Merriam-Webster's 2025 Word of the Year — this isn't a fringe concept, it's the
dominant term for exactly this failure mode.)

Read that definition again next to the AI Transformation draft: superficially competent sentences,
zero underlying substance, generic and repetitive across nine sections restating the same four
claims. **Keyword-stuffed SEO copy and AI slop are the same failure pattern wearing two different
decades' clothes** — both are content produced for a non-human evaluator (the 2012 keyword
algorithm, the 2025 content-mill prompt) rather than for the person who has to read it. VECTO can't
credibly promise "we prevent AI slop" while the content bringing people to that promise *is* slop,
just the older variety.

This argument sits above the technical citations below because it doesn't depend on winning any of them.
It isn't a question of whose discipline governs — it's whether the site's own front door contradicts
what's being sold behind it. Per the `ia` positioning principle that's a positioning question, and
it binds every draft including design's own copy.

It also dissolves the tension it looks like it creates: discoverable-via-search and credible-to-a-human
were never actually in conflict. Google's current doctrine (helpful-content, E-E-A-T,
scaled-content-abuse — all below) has moved *toward* rewarding exactly the specific, first-hand,
human-centric content the positioning already requires. Nobody is being asked to trade rankings for
brand. The two goals converged; what hasn't caught up is the briefing method on both sides.

## 1. Keyword stuffing — named and defined by Google, not a matter of opinion

Google's Search Essentials spam policy defines it directly: *"filling a web page with keywords or
numbers in an attempt to manipulate rankings in Google Search results. Often these keywords appear
in a list or group, unnaturally, or out of context… Repeating the same words or phrases so often
that it sounds unnatural."* This is a **named, documented spam violation** — not a style
preference. ([Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies))

**Where it applies:** the AI Transformation draft has nine separate H2s that all restate "AI
Transformation" verbatim ("Our AI Transformation Services," "AI Transformation Solutions for
Different Business Needs," "Why VECTO AI Transformation Services Deliver Better Operational
Results," and so on), plus a secondary-keyword list at the top of the brief that shows up
near-verbatim in the body. That's the pattern Google names explicitly.

## 2. Keyword density is a myth Google has been denying for a decade — this discredits the brief format itself

Google's John Mueller has stated repeatedly, publicly, for roughly ten years: there is no target
keyword density, no "optimal percentage" — the idea doesn't correspond to anything the ranking
systems do. His actual guidance is the opposite of a density target: use the term where it's
genuinely useful (titles, headings a reader will scan), and using it *too often* "can make the page
seem spammy and reduce its overall quality."
([Search Engine Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-keyword-density-seo-32683.html))

**Where it applies:** the brief itself opens with "Word count: ~1200" and a
target-keyword-plus-secondary-keywords list — the production method that generated the wall of
text. This is worth naming directly: the *methodology*, not just the output, is built on a premise
Google has been on record rejecting for a decade. If they're still briefing to a
density/keyword-count model, that's the real thing to raise — the bad copy is just the symptom.

## 3. "Helpful, reliable, people-first content" — Google's own self-assessment checklist

This is Google's official guidance for what their ranking systems are designed to reward, framed as
three questions to ask of any content: **who** created it (and do they have real expertise), **how**
was it made (for people first, or for search engines first), and **why** was it made (to genuinely
help someone, or to attract search traffic). Google says this directly: content should not be
"created primarily to attract search engine visits."
([Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content))

**Where it applies:** you can run either draft through Google's own three questions and it fails
visibly — the "why" is transparently "to rank for these keywords," not "to help a founder
understand what we do." This gives you a script: *"Let's run this against Google's own Who/How/Why
test before it goes further."*

## 4. E-E-A-T — why genericness is a ranking problem, not just a design one

Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trust — from Google's Search Quality Rater Guidelines, the
manual used to train the systems behind actual rankings. The "Experience" component specifically
rewards content that demonstrates real, first-hand experience with the topic. Google's 2026 guidance
sharpens this further, explicitly targeting "low-quality AI-generated pages designed only to rank"
and requiring that "AI should assist, not replace, human expertise and originality."
([Search Quality Rater Guidelines PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/hsw-sqrg.pdf);
[2026 summary](https://theguidex.com/google-quality-rater-guidelines-summary/))

**Where it applies:** the "AI Solutions and Automation Projects" section literally says the
portfolio includes "automation implementations, AI workflow systems, intelligent operational tools"
— zero first-hand specificity, nothing that couldn't be pasted onto any competitor's page. That's a
direct E-E-A-T failure by Google's own criteria, not just thin writing.

## 5. Scaled content abuse — the newest and sharpest one, and it's method-agnostic

A named, current Google spam policy (active through the 2024–2026 update cycle): *"Generating many
pages primarily to manipulate search rankings, with little or no value added for users."* Google is
explicit that it doesn't matter whether the pages are written by hand, by AI, or scraped — what
matters is the pattern: heavy templating, token-swapped variations, little unique value per page.
([policy summary](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/scaled-content-abuse-google-march-update-ai-pages-decimated))

**Where it applies:** the sub-service description doc is 36 blurbs that all follow one identical
sentence template ("We [verb] [X], [Y], [Z] and [W] for/to [benefit]") — that's the "heavily
templated, token-swapped" pattern by name. It's also the strongest card against any future push to
spin up near-identical city or industry pages beyond what the IA already scopes as legitimate (the
footer-only Service Area pages) — point at this policy *before* that conversation happens, not
after they've drafted forty of them.

## Framing a specific disagreement

Name the policy and the line, not the impression. A few phrasings that keep the conversation on the
source:

- *"Google's own spam policy names this pattern — repeating the same phrase across headers — as
  keyword stuffing. What's the reasoning for structuring it this way?"*
- *"The brief specifies a word count and a keyword list, not a set of things the page needs to say.
  Google's own guidance says there's no target density and treats content written primarily for
  search engines, not people, as the thing their systems are built to demote. Can we brief the next
  one around search intent and subtopics instead?"*
- *"This reads like it could sit on any competitor's site unchanged — that's a direct E-E-A-T
  problem (Google's own ranking-guideline framework), not just a tone issue. What's specific to
  VECTO in here?"*
- *"This has the shape scaled content abuse — Google's current spam policy — describes: one
  template, values swapped in. If we're going to do industry or sub-service pages at volume, I want
  a plan for how each one clears that bar before we start, not after."*

## The standing practice this implies

Brief and self-check every page against Google's own three questions — who made it, how, and why — before
a word count or keyword list is set. If "why was this made" can't be answered with something other than
"to rank for X," the brief isn't finished yet. The test applies to whoever wrote the brief, whichever
function that was.

## 1. Where this lands

**The findings fall into two groups, and they need different responses.**

### Group one: out of date, and the timeline explains it

Wrong URLs, service lists that don't match the site, page sections missing or invented. Most of these
drafts were written before the IA was settled, so they couldn't have been written against it. That isn't
a criticism — it's a sequencing accident. But the IA and the wireframe have both been shared since, and
the drafts haven't been revised, so these are now **a refresh task with a known answer** rather than open
questions.

### Group two: how the writing was approached, which the timeline doesn't touch

Headings built by repeating the target phrase. Paragraphs padded to reach a length. One sentence template
applied across every sibling item. No specifics anywhere. Trust claims with no evidence beneath them.
Production notes left in the copy. **None of that depends on knowing the IA.** A page written before its
URL was fixed can still be written for a person rather than for a string.

This second group is the reason for the review. Stated plainly, because hedging it would waste everyone's
time: the writing in these drafts reads generic and cheap. That's a positioning problem rather than a
style preference — section 2 explains why, and section 4 shows Google's current guidance pointing the same
direction.

The capability is clearly there. The Product Design draft contains the best sentence in all seven documents
— *"We do not design isolated screens. We design digital products that are ready for development, user
adoption and long-term improvement."* That's someone with an opinion, writing. The method is what's wrong,
not the ability.

**One finding the timeline doesn't explain either.** The AI Transformation draft and the Services hub draft
describe VECTO's AI services differently from each other. Whichever was written first, two of our own
documents currently disagree about what we sell.

### And one proposal, which is the most consequential thing in this review

Separate from the six page drafts, there's a set of **47 sub-service descriptions** proposed to go on the
landing page verbatim. This one isn't a writing problem at all — that document is the cleanest and most
taxonomy-accurate of the seven. It's a **capability-led inventory proposed for the most outcome-led page on
the site**, and it collides head-on with a principle the IA has stated in writing since the redesign began:
*"Outcome-led, not capability-led. Every hub and page leads with what changes for the client, not what
category of work it represents."* Forty-seven entries describing categories of work we perform, none
describing what changes for the reader, on the one page whose job is to make a stranger feel understood.

That's a category error rather than a quality problem, and it's the one thing in this review that isn't up
for discussion — the redesign exists because the old site led with our inventory. Full reasoning in section
6; the decision and where the content does belong are at the top of section 8.

## 2. What the website has to do

The redesign exists to serve a specific set of people making a specific decision. Every judgment in this
review follows from these goals, so they're worth stating before any criticism lands.

**The site has one job: turn a stranger's search into a conversation with us.** Search brings the reader.
The reader decides whether to make contact. Both halves matter, and as section 4 shows, they are not in
tension.

**Three goals, plus a precondition.**

1. **Human-centric.** The IA was rebuilt around specific people — primarily a non-technical founder
   evaluating an outsourcing partner for the first time, secondarily a scale-up product lead doing
   technical due diligence — and around their actual discovery and decision paths. Every page, label and
   CTA is written for the founder first. Jargon is a conversion killer.

2. **Trust-building.** The founder's real question is never "what services do you offer?" It's "can I
   trust these people with my money and my timeline?" Content earns that with specifics: named work, real
   numbers, honest limits, visible process. Claims without evidence spend trust rather than build it.

3. **Premium feel.** Premium isn't decoration — it reads as restraint. Fewer sections that say more. A
   page with a point of view. The opposite of premium isn't ugly; it's *interchangeable*.

4. **Discoverable** — the precondition, not a fourth priority. The site must rank or none of the above
   gets read. Section 4 makes the case that the conflict between ranking and readability is largely
   illusory under Google's current guidance — but "largely" is the honest word, and I can't assess the
   ranking effect of any specific change in this review. Where a rewrite here drops a phrase, treat the
   ranking question as open and measurable rather than settled. Agreeing what we watch, and from what
   baseline, is item 9 in section 8.

**The constraint that follows from all of it.** VECTO sells the ability to use AI without the output
degrading into [AI slop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_slop) — generic, repetitive, superficially
competent text with nothing underneath. Content on our own site that is generic, repetitive and
substance-free disproves the pitch in the reader's hands, whatever it does for rankings. So content that
contradicts the positioning is turned down on positioning grounds. That's a standing project rule, not a
per-document preference, and it binds everyone's drafts including mine.

In practice it has cost nothing so far: every objection in section 6 is also a failure against Google's
own published guidance.

**One structural fact worth repeating.** The site's page structure is already specified. Every URL has a
table in the IA saying which sections it has, in what order, and why. Copy fills those sections. Copy that
invents its own structure isn't a copy revision, it's a structural change, and it goes through the IA
rather than around it.

## 3. The standard being applied

Twenty-two checks — six for a brief, sixteen for the copy itself. They're named rather than numbered
below so this review can refer to them in plain English. The maintained version lives in our
content-standards document; if the two ever disagree, that one wins.

**The one-line version of all of it:** would this page still make sense, and still help the reader, if you
deleted the target keyword from the brief entirely? Good SEO content survives that. Copy built around the
keyword falls apart without it.

### What a brief has to do, before anyone writes to it

| Check | What it means |
|---|---|
| **States the search intent** | One sentence on what the reader is trying to find out or do |
| **Lists the questions to answer** | The real subtopics the page has to cover, not just a keyword |
| **Names the page's sections** | Points at the IA table for that URL and says which sections the copy fills |
| **Uses the real URL** | Matched against the IA, including sub-service nesting — not eyeballed |
| **Sets no quotas** | No keyword density target, no repetition count, no headings mandated to contain the exact phrase. A word count, if given at all, is an estimate of the output — never a target to hit |
| **Cites references we'd stand behind** | Two or three pages to out-explain, each one able to pass the copy checks below itself |

### What the copy has to do

**On substance**

| Check | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Survives losing the keyword** | Delete the target phrase throughout. Does the page still say something? |
| **One specific per section** | A number, a named technology, a named project, a real constraint, or a first-hand detail. If a section could be pasted onto a competitor's site unchanged, it fails |
| **Every claim supported** | Anything asserted about results, client sentiment or scale is backed on the page or links to the evidence |
| **Says something only we could say** | Google's own test, quoted in section 4: is this closer to *"7 Tips for First-Time Homebuyers"* or to *"Why We Waived the Inspection & Saved Money"*? |

**On structure**

| Check | What it means |
|---|---|
| **One idea per section** | Summarise each section in one clause. Duplicate clauses get cut, not renamed |
| **Headings answer real questions** | Read the headings alone as a list. Do they read as a table of contents, or as a phrase being repeated? |
| **FAQs are real objections** | Distinct questions a sceptical buyer actually asks. No two asking about the same thing; no "what is X" answered by restating X |
| **Matches the page's specified structure** | Diff the section list against the IA table. Both missing sections and invented ones are findings |

**On voice**

| Check | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Survives being read aloud** | Read it out. Flag anything you would never actually say to someone |
| **Plain language first** | Technical depth second. Would a non-technical founder understand this on first read? |
| **No repeated sentence template** | Line the sibling items up. If the only thing changing is the nouns, rewrite them |
| **Speaks to a person** | Second person, specific. Count the sentences whose subject is "businesses" rather than "you" |

**On delivery hygiene**

| Check | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Names match the IA** | Service, sub-service and industry names, counts and groupings match exactly. Invented or merged entries fail even when they read better |
| **Metadata a human would click** | Title and description are sentences, unique per page, not truncated, not pipe-separated keyword lists |
| **Links resolve** | Every URL paste-matched against the IA, with naturally varied anchor text |
| **Delivered clean** | No production notes, placeholder text, reference links or competitor links left in the copy |

**Which of these belong to which group** (from section 1). Five depend on having the IA — *names the page's
sections*, *uses the real URL*, *matches the page's specified structure*, *names match the IA*, and *links
resolve*. Those are the out-of-date group, and the answers are all available now. **The other seventeen
don't depend on the IA at all.** A page can pass every one of them without knowing a single URL. Those are
the ones to read closely.

**Where each check comes from, stated honestly.** Most trace directly to something Google publishes, and
section 4 gives the source and date for each: *survives losing the keyword*, *one specific per section*,
*says something only we could say*, *one idea per section*, *headings answer real questions*, *sets no
quotas*, *cites references we'd stand behind*, and *names match the IA* all follow from the spam policies,
the helpful-content page or the generative-AI guide.

Four do **not** come from Google, and shouldn't borrow its authority: *survives being read aloud*, *plain
language first*, *no repeated sentence template*, and *speaks to a person*. Those are house standards —
they come from the positioning in section 2, which is a call we've made rather than a rule Google publishes.
Same for the *premium feel* goal. They're no less binding for that, but they're arguable on their own
merits rather than by citation, and anyone is entitled to argue them. (One boundary case: the
keyword-density point is Google's position stated verbally many times over a decade rather than written on
a documentation page — the documented equivalent is the keyword-stuffing spam policy.)

## 4. Where search guidance actually stands in 2026

Sections 4.1 to 4.3 are Google's own currently-published documentation, each with the date the page was
last updated. Sections 4.4 and 4.5 go beyond Google into third-party research and my own judgment, and
say so.

### 4.1 The single most useful thing Google has published recently

In May 2026 Google published [*Optimizing your website for generative AI features on Google
Search*](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide) (last updated
**10 July 2026**;
[announcement](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2026/05/a-new-resource-for-optimizing), 15 May
2026). Its position on the entire "optimise for AI" category is blunt:

> "From Google Search's perspective, optimizing for generative AI search is optimizing for the search
> experience, and thus still SEO."

The same guide has a *Mythbusting* section naming things that don't help: `llms.txt` and AI-specific
markup ("Google Search ignores them"), "chunking" content for machines, rewriting content for AI systems,
seeking inauthentic mentions, and **"overfocusing on structured data."** What it says *does* drive
inclusion in AI answers is being crawlable, being snippet-eligible, and publishing **non-commodity
content**. Google's own worked example of that distinction:

> commodity — *"7 Tips for First-Time Homebuyers"*
> non-commodity — *"Why We Waived the Inspection & Saved Money: A Look Inside the Sewer Line"*

That contrast is the most useful editorial test available right now. It is also the entire argument for
specificity, made by Google rather than by a designer.

**A note that concerns us directly.** We sell GEO as a service. Google's guide says GEO *is* SEO and
explicitly disavows most of the tactic stack sold under that name. So our own GEO page has to be careful:
if it promises `llms.txt` files and content chunking, it's selling something Google documents as ignored,
which fails our own *every claim supported* and *says something only we could say* checks on our own site.
Visibility inside ChatGPT, Perplexity and Copilot is a real surface that Google's guidance doesn't govern,
so the service isn't empty — it's the tactic list where the credibility risk sits.

### 4.2 What Google says it rewards

- [*Creating helpful, reliable,
  people-first content*](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)
  (last updated **10 December 2025**) remains the operative standard. Its self-assessment asks whether
  content offers "original information, reporting, research, or analysis", whether it shows "first-hand
  expertise", and whether it would satisfy someone who arrived directly rather than via search.

- **Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness and Trust (E-E-A-T)** comes from the [Search Quality Rater
  Guidelines](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/guidelines.raterhub.com/en//searchqualityevaluatorguidelines.pdf),
  current version dated **11 September 2025**. Two details are worth knowing precisely, because both get
  misquoted constantly: Google states that **"trust is most important"**, and also that **"E-E-A-T itself
  isn't a specific ranking factor."** It describes what the systems are built to approximate, not a dial
  you turn. The guidelines are revised periodically, so treat the September 2025 version as the one to
  check rather than assuming permanence.

- The [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) (last updated
  **15 May 2026**) name **scaled content abuse**: "when many pages are generated for the primary purpose
  of manipulating search rankings and not helping users… large amounts of unoriginal content that provides
  little to no value to users, **no matter how it's created**." Method-agnostic by design — hand-written,
  AI-written and scraped are treated identically. **Keyword stuffing** and **doorway abuse** are
  separately named violations on the same page.

- Recent [update history](https://developers.google.com/search/updates/ranking): core updates on 11
  December 2025, 27 March 2026 and 21 May 2026; spam updates on 24 March 2026 and 24 June 2026. No new
  *ranking* guidance was published alongside any of them ([Search Engine
  Land](https://searchengineland.com/google-march-2026-core-update-rollout-is-now-complete-473883), April
  2026), so the December 2025 helpful-content page is still the standard for what to write.

### 4.3 What has been retired, with receipts

| Practice | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Word-count targets** | No preferred length exists | [Helpful-content page](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) (10 Dec 2025): *"Are you writing to a particular word count because you've heard or read that Google has a preferred word count? (No, we don't.)"* Reinforced 10 July 2026: "There's no ideal page length" |
| **Keyword density targets** | No such factor | Stated by Google's John Mueller repeatedly for roughly a decade — e.g. [Search Engine Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-keyword-density-seo-32683.html). Honest caveat: the primary artefact is verbal Q&A rather than a documentation page; the documented equivalent is the keyword-stuffing spam policy |
| **Exact-match repetition and long-tail variant coverage** | Unnecessary | [AI features guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide) (10 July 2026): "AI systems can understand synonyms… you don't have to worry that you don't have enough 'long-tail' keywords," and relevance is understood "even when there is no exact match between the query and the page's primary content" |
| **"More pages means more traffic"** | Counterproductive, and a policy violation at volume | Same page: "a high quantity of pages doesn't make a website higher quality or more relevant to users" — and producing them for query variants "violates Google's scaled content abuse spam policy" |
| **The meta keywords tag** | Ignored since 2009 | [Meta tags Google supports](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/special-tags) (page updated 10 Dec 2025): "The meta-keyword tag is not used by Google Search, and it has no effect on indexing and ranking at all" |
| **Near-duplicate city or variant landing pages** | Named violation: doorway abuse | [Spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) (15 May 2026) |
| **Refreshing dates to look current** | Listed among things that don't help | [Helpful-content page](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) (10 Dec 2025), in the self-assessment questions, answered "(No, it won't)" |

There's also a Google page specifically about buying SEO help, and it cuts both ways: [*Guidance on using
third-party SEO tools, services, and
advice*](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/third-party-seo) (last updated **5 June
2026**) states that "Third-party tools don't have access to our internal ranking data. They can't
guarantee performance." That's why this review asks for reasoning against Google's documentation rather
than against tool scores — and equally why nobody here, me very much included, should assert what a
specific change will do to rankings. Where a ranking outcome comes up below, treat it as a question to
settle with Search Console data rather than a conclusion this review has reached.

### 4.4 What genuinely changed recently

- **AI Overviews reduce clicks on the queries where they appear.** [Pew Research
  Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/)
  (22 July 2025; 900 US adults on a probability-based panel, browsing tracked across roughly 68,000–69,000
  Google searches in March 2025): people clicked a traditional result on **8%** of searches that showed an
  AI summary, versus **15%** of those that didn't. Clicks on links *inside* the summaries were **1%**.

- **The aggregate picture is genuinely contested.** Google's own post ([Liz Reid,
  6 August
  2025](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/ai-search-driving-more-queries-higher-quality-clicks/))
  says total organic click volume is "relatively stable year-over-year" with higher click quality, and
  attributes third-party alarm to flawed methodology — without publishing supporting data in that post.
  The defensible position: per-query click-through on AI-Overview searches is clearly down in independent
  measurement, while whole-site traffic effects are disputed. Don't plan on either extreme.

- **The mechanism matters for how you write.** The same generative-AI guide discloses
  retrieval-augmented grounding — the model answers by fetching live pages and summarising them rather
  than from what it memorised — plus **query fan-out**: one typed question becomes several related
  sub-queries behind the scenes, each pulling its own sources. The practical consequence is that covering
  a topic properly beats targeting a string, because you're being matched against sub-questions nobody
  typed.

- **Measurement.** Search Console now has generative-AI performance reports
  ([announced 3 June 2026](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2026/06/gen-ai-performance-reports);
  data from 18 May 2026, no backfill, impressions only). Free, first-party, and the only honest source
  for this, since no third-party tool has access to Google's ranking data.

- **On AI-written content performing worse.** The most-cited study is
  [Graphite's](https://graphite.io/five-percent/research/ai-content-in-search-and-llms) (October 2025,
  31,493 keywords), finding 86% of articles ranking in Google are human-written and 82% of
  ChatGPT/Perplexity citations are human-written. Treat it as suggestive rather than proof: it uses
  detector-based classification, it's correlational, it comes from a growth agency, and it explicitly
  did **not** test AI-drafted-then-human-edited content, which the authors say may perform better. The
  honest reading is that AI as a drafting tool is fine and AI as a publishing pipeline is what gets
  penalised — which is exactly Google's "no matter how it's created" wording.

### 4.5 Which of this applies to a site like ours

Most published SEO advice is written for high-volume content operations — publishers and marketplaces
running hundreds of pages a month. This site isn't that. It's a services site with a fixed page inventory,
where real updates (blog posts, case studies) land roughly every two weeks and new service categories
rarely. That difference changes which advice applies.

**Applies, and plays to our advantage:** first-hand, non-commodity content — the people delivering the
work are the ones with something specific to say, which is exactly what Google's guidance now rewards;
named authorship and real bios; internal linking so pages are findable; page performance; a modest amount
of structured data for rich-result eligibility; Search Console as the single source of measurement truth.

**Doesn't apply, and should be declined:** publishing at volume to cover query variants (actively
policy-violating per the July 2026 guide); crawl-budget work (Google's own crawl-budget documentation
scopes it to very large, frequently-updated sites, and ours is neither); paid AI-visibility tracking
suites; `llms.txt` and chunking programmes.

## 5. The reference pages cited in the drafts

Three external pages are cited inside the drafts as references for section design:
[WebFX's AI-search-optimization service page](https://www.webfx.com/seo/services/ai-search-optimization/)
(cited in the Marketing draft), [Renderforest's YouTube-video-ideas
page](https://www.renderforest.com/youtube-video-ideas) (cited in the Marketing, Marketing Strategy and
Graphic Design drafts), and
[ScienceSoft's software-development services page](https://www.scnsoft.com/software-development/services)
(cited in the AI Transformation, Product Design and Graphic Design drafts). The first two were read
closely on 5 August 2026; everything quoted below is verbatim from those live pages as of that date. **The
ScienceSoft page was not assessed** — so nothing below is a finding against it, and the request in section
8 to replace references applies to it only in the sense that every reference should be able to pass the
checks in section 3.

Neither of the two that were read is a credible design reference for this site, and the reason isn't
aesthetic.

### WebFX

Roughly sixteen stacked sections and about 2,300 words, plus a navigation and footer link farm of 200-odd
links; the proposal CTA appears seven times. Its title tag and its H1 don't match, which is a deliberate
keyword-coverage play. Headings repeat the target phrase mechanically — *"Your search for AI search
optimization services ends here"*. Four of its eleven FAQ entries ask about the same thing for keyword
coverage: *"What are generative engine optimization services?"*, *"What is generative engine
optimization?"*, *"What is a generative engine optimization agency?"*, *"What does a generative engine
optimization agency do?"* There's an unrendered shortcode leaking into the live page
(`[fx-form 211035815147045 version="popup-form-proposal" manual-load=true]`) and icon alt-text baked into
H3s.

**Credit where it's due:** it publishes real pricing (*"$3,000 /month"*, tiers up to $68,250), names a
case study with a number (*"Thompson Tractor / 586% Increase in Traffic From Generative AI Sources"*), and
has one clear conversion goal. As a lead-generation machine it's competent. As a *design* reference it
teaches keyword-echoed headings, FAQ padding, badge clutter and endless section stacking.

### Renderforest

This one isn't a matter of taste, because its defects are checkable facts.

It still ships a `meta-keywords` tag stuffed with eight variants including *"youtube video ideas for
girls"* — a tag Google has documented as having no effect since 2009. Five of its tip headings are
literally "1." through "5." with no text. One heading is a broken dynamic string: *"Be the Next&nbsp;&nbsp;
To Generate Your YouTube Video Ideas"*. On a page about *video ideas*, four of the five tips are about
intro videos. The testimonials have swapped roles — *"Paul, DIY Youtuber"* describes "lifestyle, beauty,
tips and shopping" while *"Laura, Beauty Vlogger"* filmed "How to Make an Art Object with Paper Mache" —
and one still cites *"Top Make-up Trends of 2021"*. The page claims *"Trusted by 34M+ users"* in the hero
and *"over 20 million users worldwide"* in its own meta description. It's fast and cleanly localised into
ten languages; that's the credit it earns.

### Why this matters beyond the references themselves

A writer told "use this as a reference" absorbs the pattern, and the transmission is visible in the
drafts. The Product Design draft's FAQ contains *"What are product design services?"*, *"What is included
in digital product design services?"* and *"Why choose VECTO for product design services?"* — the same
near-duplicate padding as the WebFX FAQ, in the same position on the page. The reference didn't just fail
to help; it taught something specific.

**What we need instead.** References should be pages we'd be happy to be compared against — agency and
product sites that explain things well. If the intent in citing these was purely structural ("we want an
FAQ accordion here, a pricing table there"), say that in the brief as a layout note. Don't cite a page
whose content pattern we'd reject, because the content pattern is what gets copied.

### The AI Transformation draft

**Verdict.** The weakest of the six, and the only one with a factual defect rather than only a stylistic
one: it invents a set of AI service names that contradicts both the IA and our own Services hub draft.

**What it gets right**

- The target URL matches the IA exactly.
- The meta title and description are readable sentences rather than pipe-separated keyword lists.
- The sub-service descriptions name real, recognisable deliverables — "approval process automation,
  document processing automation", "AI readiness assessments, operational bottleneck analysis" — which is
  more concrete than most of the other five manage.
- The FAQ answer on timelines refuses the easy promise: "Smaller automation systems can launch quickly,
  while enterprise implementations require longer deployment phases."

**What it gets wrong**

**The AI service list is invented, and contradicts our own Services hub draft.** The IA specifies five AI
sub-services: *AI Integration · Generative AI Development · ML Engineering · Data Analytics · AI Prompt
Engineering*. The Services hub draft uses those five correctly. This draft replaces them with six
inventions: "AI Transformation Strategy and Consulting", "AI Process Automation", "AI-Powered Customer
Experience Solutions", "AI Data Analysis and Decision Support", "AI Integration Into Existing Business
Systems", "Custom AI Solutions Development". Set those against the brief's own secondary keyword list —
*ai transformation services · ai transformation consulting services · ai consulting · ai consulting
services · ai process automation · ai solutions* — and the overlap is hard to miss; "AI Process
Automation" is verbatim from it. These aren't services we sell.

**Twelve of the page's sixteen top-level sections repeat the exact phrase "AI Transformation" in their
heading** — the H1 plus eleven below it. Read as a list: "Our AI Transformation Services", "AI

is a metronome. The deeper issue is the comma lists doing the work of specificity: "customer support,
internal operations, sales, communication and task management" (Bot Development, five items); "external
platforms, APIs, payment systems, CRMs, analytics tools and other business services" (Third-Party
Integrations, six); "hosting environments, servers, backups, deployments, domains, databases and
infrastructure-related technical requirements" (Hosting, seven). Breadth of related nouns is standing in
for one concrete claim — which is the same failure as keyword repetition wearing different clothes: it
reads as coverage rather than as knowledge.

**Nothing in 47 entries is specific to us.** No figure, no named client, no named project, and — apart from
"iOS and Android" — no named technology. Every entry would sit unchanged on any competitor's site. For the
footer or a card grid that's survivable. On the landing page it's the opposite of the *says something only
we could say* test.

### Why it can't go on the landing page

**The first reason is the whole reason, and it's already written down.** The IA's guiding principles include
this, verbatim, and have from the start of the redesign:

> **Outcome-led, not capability-led.** Every hub and page leads with what changes for the client, not what
> category of work it represents.

Forty-seven entries of the form "We [verb] [noun list]" is the definition of capability-led. Every single
one describes a category of work we perform. Not one describes what changes for the person reading it. And
the page proposed for it is the one page on the site where that principle matters most — the first thing a
stranger sees, whose entire job is to make a person feel understood rather than to prove we have a wide
menu.

That's the objection. It isn't about writing quality, it isn't about SEO tactics, and it isn't a matter of
taste that can be split down the middle: the redesign's whole premise is that this site earns trust by
leading with the client's problem, and this proposal leads with our inventory. The two can't both sit in the
same section.

The three reasons below all follow from that one. They're worth stating because they're independently
checkable, but none of them is the argument — the principle is.

**It replaces the homepage's job.** The IA gives the homepage's services section the six-stage
transformation grid — *Discover · Design · Build · Grow · Scale · Maintain* — with AI as its own band
immediately below. That grid exists so a non-technical founder can locate themselves in about ten seconds:
*I'm at the stage where I don't know if this is worth building yet.* A list of 47 services asks a reader who
doesn't yet know what they need to self-diagnose from a catalogue. It's more information and less help — and
a founder who can't place themselves on a page leaves it.

**It displaces the argument the page is making.** At roughly 25 words each, 47 blurbs is about 1,200 words in
one section. Everything the IA puts after the services grid — the benefits, industries, company stages,
portfolio, reviews, process, engagement models, the estimate on-ramp, the FAQ — moves down behind a wall of
list. Those blocks are the trust sequence. The homepage stops being an argument and becomes a directory,
which is precisely the "premium reads as restraint" point in section 2 running in reverse.

**And in SEO terms it works against us, which is worth knowing since that's presumably the upside being
sought.** The IA specifies a page per sub-service. Each of those is the thing that should rank for its own
term, and each has room for what actually converts: deliverables, timeline, team, sample work, pricing
context. Putting all 47 terms on the homepage sets our strongest page against 47 weaker ones for the same
queries. The homepage usually wins that — so the sub-service pages get suppressed by our own hand, and the
visitor arrives somewhere that mentions their need in one sentence instead of the page built to answer it.
Google's current guidance says the same thing from the other direction: *"a high quantity of pages doesn't
make a website higher quality or more relevant to users,"* and relevance is now understood *"even when there
is no exact match between the query and the page's primary content"* (generative-AI guide, 10 July 2026).
Coverage on one page is not what earns a term any more. The page that answers the question is.

**The full taxonomy already has three homes, by design** — each service page carries its own category's full
list, the Services hub shows a representative selection per stage card, and the footer carries the complete
taxonomy as crawlable links from every page, homepage included. This document is the right content for all
three. It isn't surplus; it's been pointed at a fourth place that already has a different job.

**None of which means the concern behind the proposal is wrong** — there probably is a real problem underneath
it, and section 8 asks which one, because the answer changes what we build instead.

**What to do with it instead**

Nothing in this document is wasted. Concretely:

- **The footer taxonomy** takes the names as-is.
- **Each service page's "what's included" cards** take that category's entries — 11 on Software
  Development, 12 on Marketing, and so on. This is what those cards are for, and this document is now the
  source for them.
- **Each sub-service page's hero** takes its entry as the starting point for the outcome line, rewritten
  from "what we do" into "what you get" — the Graphic Design rewrite in the previous section shows the
  move.
- **The homepage** takes six stage descriptions, which don't exist yet and are a much smaller writing job
  than this document already represents.

**Rewritten passage — one entry, re-pointed**

> **Before** (as written, for a catalogue)
> *Bot Development* — "We create intelligent chatbots and automation bots for customer support, internal
> operations, sales, communication and task management."

> **After** (as a sub-service page hero, addressed to a buyer)
> **Bot Development**
> Most support tickets are the same eight questions. A bot that handles those well is worth more than one
> that attempts everything badly — so we start by reading your actual ticket history and finding the
> repeatable share, then build for that and hand the rest to a human cleanly.
> *[to supply: the share of volume a typical build absorbs, from a project we've done.]*

Same service, same length. The first tells you the category exists. The second tells you how we think, and
would be hard for a competitor to copy without doing the work.

## 7. Patterns across all seven

Seven observations that aren't about any single document. These are the ones worth fixing at source,
because each is currently being reproduced page by page.

**1. What the brief leaves out explains most of this — and it isn't the word count.** Five of the six page
briefs open with a target keyword, a secondary-keyword list and a word count. That's the whole brief.
Nothing states what the reader came to find out, what questions the page has to answer, or which sections
the copy fills. Note the honest version, though: the Services hub draft has *no* word count and still
carries the most findings by volume, so removing length targets alone wouldn't have fixed it. A length
target is what fills the vacuum when nothing else specifies what the page must contain. See the closing
note to this section for the limits of that as a defence.

**2. The sub-service descriptions are the natural experiment that proves point 1.** That document had no
keyword brief at all — no target keyword, no secondary list, no word count — and it is the cleanest and
most taxonomy-accurate of the seven, with nothing left in it that shouldn't ship. Same people, no keyword
to serve, better output. That's the strongest available evidence that the brief format is producing the
padding and the keyword-echo headings, not the writers. It's also the reason to fix the brief before
asking anyone to rewrite anything.

**3. No figure, named client or dated project appears as evidence anywhere in the seven documents**, and —
apart from "iOS and Android" — no named technology either. Not one, across all of them: seven documents
describing a company that has actually done the work. The closest any of them gets is "a few weeks." This
is the single biggest gap between these documents and the standard, and it's the thing Google's current
guidance is most explicit about: "original information… first-hand expertise", non-commodity content. Some
of the raw material genuinely isn't written down anywhere yet, and section 8 collects it — but note that
the drafts don't ask for it either, except in the three "integrate real reviews" notes. A draft that can't
get a number should say so in the margin rather than papering over the hole with "measurable results."

**4. The differentiator is missing from every page.** The IA states one per service: marketing by the
people who built your product; design as risk reduction, with a framing stage; AI used inside our own
delivery so clients get it cheaper. The copy states none of them, on any page. What's left is the generic
category description, which is exactly why every page reads interchangeably.

**5. Three blocks are repeated across pages.** The five company stages appear in the Marketing, Marketing
Strategy and Services hub drafts; the thirteen industries with the identical ScienceSoft reference appear in
the AI Transformation, Product Design and Graphic Design drafts; an evidence-free "Trusted by…" heading
appears in four of the six page drafts. The company-stage axis already has its own dedicated section of the
site, one page per stage — replicating it competes with the pages built for it, and gives three service
pages the same middle.

**6. The pages are converging on one silhouette.** Hero → trust strip → service list → benefits → process →
company stages → industries → portfolio → testimonials → related services → blog → FAQ, largely regardless
of what the page is for. Not every element is on every page — the Marketing Strategy and Graphic Design
drafts have no portfolio block, three of the six have the stages block — but the shape is the same, and the
IA gives these URLs deliberately *different* shapes. Worth naming for the road ahead rather than as an
accusation about these six: uniform templating with values swapped in, at volume, is what Google's
scaled-content-abuse policy describes. Six pages is not that. The forty-odd sub-service pages the IA
specifies, all built this way, would be — so the habit is the thing to change now, while it's cheap.

**7. The subject of nearly every sentence is "we" or "businesses".** "Businesses choose VECTO because…",
"We help businesses…" — and all 47 sub-service blurbs open with "We" or "Our team". The reader is being
described rather than addressed. The fix is mechanical and improves every page: make "you" the subject and
see which sentences survive.

**And plainly in the other direction:** the taxonomy work is largely accurate and in the sub-service
descriptions it's essentially perfect; the metadata reads as human sentences rather than keyword lists in
every draft (the Marketing Strategy truncation aside); several FAQ sets show real judgment about what a
buyer worries about; the agency-versus-freelancer section is a section the IA should have had and didn't;
the sub-service descriptions are the only document that ships clean; and the Product Design draft contains
sentences with a point of view.

The capability is evidently there. What's missing is the method: the briefs carry a keyword, a keyword list
and a length, and the drafts execute exactly that. To be clear that this is an explanation rather than an
excuse — an under-specified brief accounts for the padding and the missing sections, but nothing in it
required the headings to repeat the target phrase, the sibling items to share one sentence shape, or a trust
block to claim what it can't show. Those were choices at the keyboard, and they're the ones to change.

## 8. What changes for round two

### First, the one decision that isn't open

**The 47 sub-service descriptions are not going on the landing page.** Not a negotiating position — the
proposal is capability-led content on the page the entire redesign was built to make outcome-led, and the IA
has said so in writing since day one. Full reasoning in section 6. Page structure is mine to decide, and
this is decided.

Being equally clear about what is *not* being said: the document is good. It's the most accurate thing
produced in this round and the only one that ships clean. It is now the **source of record** for four places
the content does belong — the footer taxonomy, each service page's "what's included" cards, each sub-service
page's hero line, and, condensed to six, the homepage's stage descriptions. Nothing here gets thrown away;
it gets routed.

**The one thing I want back before anything else moves:** which problem the proposal was solving. If it was
*"these 47 services need to be crawlable and indexed from the homepage,"* that's a legitimate concern with a
better answer, and the conversation to have is about the footer and the internal-linking model — the footer
already links all 47 from every page including the homepage. If it was *"visitors can't see the full range
of what we do,"* that's a real problem too, and the answer is the Services hub, which exists for exactly
that and currently isn't built to do it well (see the Services hub findings in section 6). Either way I want
to solve the actual problem. What I won't do is solve it on the homepage's main services section.

### Then, the work — split by who owns what

Two things gate the rest: **item 5**, because no page can carry a real claim until the specifics are
collected, and **item 9**, because we shouldn't ship a rewritten page without having agreed what we're
watching. Everything else can start now — the IA and the wireframe are already shared, so the out-of-date
refresh has no dependency, and the writing-method change never had one.

### Content and SEO owns

**1. Brief properly.** Every brief states the search intent in a sentence, lists the questions and subtopics
the page must answer, names the target URL, and names which sections of that page the copy fills. Word count
becomes an estimate of the output rather than a target. No density guidance, no headings mandated to contain
the exact phrase.

**2. Write to the specified page structure** (supplied per item 6). A missing section, or an added one, is a
structural change — raise it as a question rather than resolving it in the draft.

**3. Refresh the out-of-date items against the IA and the wireframe.** These drafts predate the IA, so this
is updating stale copy rather than correcting judgment: `/services/marketing-strategy` →
`/services/marketing/marketing-strategy`; `/services/graphic-design` → `/services/marketing/graphic-design`;
the two invented internal link targets in the Marketing draft; and the AI Transformation draft's six
invented service names reconciled to the five in the IA and in the Services hub draft.

**4. Housekeeping on delivery.** Replace the reference pages with ones that could pass these checks
themselves — and if a reference is only about layout, label it as a layout note. Keep production notes and
placeholders out of the copy body; they're useful, they just need to travel in a comment or a separate
section. Mark heading levels explicitly, as the Product Design draft does.

### Design and IA owns — mine

**5. Supply the specifics.** Nobody can invent a real number, and the briefs gave nothing to work with. Per
service: two or three named projects with a real starting point and a real outcome figure; the tools and
platforms we actually use; typical timelines, team sizes and roles; the deliverables list per sub-service;
and the real reviews — the note "integrate real reviews from GMB profile" appears three times across these
drafts, which is the drafts asking for exactly this.

**6. Put the page structure in the brief itself**, per URL, rather than relying on the IA and wireframe
being available somewhere. Both are shared, but the drafts don't reflect them — which is equally consistent
with the IA not existing yet when they were written and with it existing but not being to hand. I'm not
going to guess which, because putting the relevant six lines at the top of each brief removes the question
either way. Same for the writing bar: the two pages to compare against are the **React technology page** and
the **Healthcare industry page** in the wireframe — I'll link them directly in the brief rather than leaving
"what good looks like" to inference.

**7. Settle the agency-versus-freelancer block, and check "Targeting".** On the first: recommendation is to
adopt it into the IA's sub-service template so every sub-service page gets one, rather than leaving it
stranded on the Graphic Design page. On the second: two documents list a marketing sub-service called
"Targeting" that the IA doesn't have, which probably means the IA is wrong rather than both documents. I'll
verify and add it if it's real — don't remove it from drafts in the meantime.

**8. Settle what our GEO service promises**, in light of Google's July 2026 guidance that the usual GEO
tactic list is ignored (section 4.1). This one shouldn't be settled without the SEO side of the room —
whoever is seeing what actually moves for clients has the better evidence, and the answer needs to hold up
on the GEO page before it gets briefed.

### Joint — item 9

**9. Agree what we measure, before round two rather than after.** Several rewrites in section 6 drop or move
a target phrase, and I can't tell you what that does to rankings — nobody can, per Google's own note on
third-party tools. So pick the baseline now: which pages, which queries, and what in Search Console we're
watching, including the generative-AI reports with their known limits (impressions only, data from 18 May
2026, no backfill). If a change costs us something measurable, I want to know from the data rather than
from an argument. This is the SEO side's call on instrumentation; I just need it agreed before we ship the
first rewritten page.

### Definition of done, for both columns

A draft is accepted when it passes the twenty-two checks in section 3, and findings come back naming the
check rather than as adjectives — so a disagreement is about a specific rule, and can be settled by changing
the rule if the rule turns out to be wrong.

Two different routes to overturning a check, depending on which kind it is (see the note at the end of
section 3). For the Google-derived ones: show a dated Google source or Search Console data that contradicts
it, and it changes. For the four house standards — *read aloud*, *plain language first*, *no repeated
template*, *speaks to a person* — there's no citation to beat, so the argument is on the merits, and it's a
conversation rather than a ruling. Those four descend from the positioning in section 2, which is mine to
own; if one of them is doing more harm than good on a specific page, say so and we'll look at that page.

### Sequencing

Items 5 and 6 land first; round-two briefs follow them; item 9 is agreed before the first rewritten page
goes live. Round two should be **one page only** — proposed: Marketing Strategy, because it's the shortest,
it's a sub-service page so it exercises the template, and it has the clearest gaps to fill. Better to agree
the standard on one page than have five come back at once.

**Still to fix on this plan:** the page for round two isn't decided until someone says so, and there are no
dates on any of it yet. Both come out of the conversation this document is meant to start — if you disagree
with Marketing Strategy as the first one, say which page and why.

## Appendix C — terms used above

- **GEO** — generative engine optimisation; work aimed at getting a brand to appear in AI-generated
  answers. Google's position (section 4.1) is that this is SEO rather than a separate discipline.
- **E-E-A-T** — experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust; the framework in Google's quality-rater
  guidelines.
- **Retrieval-augmented grounding** — the AI answers a question by fetching live pages and summarising them,
  rather than from what the model memorised during training. It's why being crawlable and specific matters
  more than being formatted for machines.
- **Query fan-out** — one typed question becomes several related sub-queries behind the scenes, each pulling
  its own sources.
- **GMB profile** — Google Business Profile, formerly Google My Business; the source of the real reviews
  three of the drafts flag as still to be integrated.
- **Snippet-eligible** — the page is allowed to have its text shown as a search snippet, which is also the
  precondition for being quoted in an AI-generated answer.
- **Doorway page** — a thin page built only to rank for one search term, with nothing distinct to offer a
  visitor who lands on it. A named spam violation.
- **Scaled content abuse** — Google's policy against generating many low-value pages primarily to
  manipulate rankings, applied regardless of whether they were written by a person, by AI, or scraped.
- **The IA** — our information-architecture document, which specifies every page's section structure. See
  the note at the top of this review.
